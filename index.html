What you already pay

The school taxes already on your bill

We already pay a lot for public education. On a home assessed at $1 million, households in the Acalanes district pay roughly $1,000 to $2,100 a year in school parcel taxes and bonds alone, before the regular property tax is counted. Measure W would add $168 more per parcel.

Every property in the district pays for schools several times over: flat parcel taxes that fall equally on a small condominium and a large estate, voter-approved bonds charged as a percentage of assessed value, and a share of the base 1 percent property tax. That is the total, before Measure W. On a home assessed at $1 million, a household here already pays between about $5,800 and $6,900 a year for public education once parcel taxes, school bonds, and the school share of the base property tax are counted together. Measure W asks for $168 more.

The wrong answer

A spending problem, not a revenue problem

The Acalanes Union High School District does not have a revenue problem. It has a spending problem. State data show the district’s cost per student roughly doubled between 2012–13 and 2024–25, rising from $10,305 to $20,508, a 99 percent increase, while Bay Area consumer prices rose about 45 percent over the same years. The money to run excellent schools is already here, and the district’s own results prove it: on the taxes we pay now, Acalanes high schools rank in the nation’s top 5 percent.

The district already collects a $301 parcel tax on every parcel with no expiration, on top of the parcel taxes homeowners pay to each K–8 feeder district and the bonds counted above. Just last year the district spent $834,000 on a standalone special election for Measure T, a $130 parcel tax that voters rejected. Eighteen months later the board is back with Measure W, a $168 parcel tax lasting eight years, nearly 30 percent larger than the tax the community already turned down. More than $68,000 has gone to Measure W polling and consultants before a single vote has been cast.

California’s current budget also handed the district a one-time grant of nearly $5 million, and its property tax revenue keeps climbing as home values rise. A parcel tax also ignores ability to pay. It charges the owner of a small condominium the same flat amount as the owner of a mansion, and it asks the more than nine in ten households with no child in these schools to pay it too. Combined with the region’s other new and proposed taxes, Measure W makes an already expensive area less affordable, and it rewards rising costs rather than requiring the district to account for them.

Where the money goes tells the story. In 2025–26 the Acalanes board approved about $2.1 million in special education contracts with out-of-state residential facilities in Utah, including $436,360 for a single placement at Crimson Heights, a program that Utah regulators had decertified. The “Citizens’ Oversight and independent audits” the Yes campaign points to did not prevent it. A district that can commit sums like these has not run out of money. It is making choices, and it is asking taxpayers to fund them without first accounting for the ones it has already made.

Point by point

Answering the Yes campaign

This is not an expense problem, it is a revenue problem. AUHSD ranks near the bottom of California districts for state and federal funding and receives about $16,082 per pupil.

AUHSD is a community-funded district, financed mainly by local property taxes that rise with home values, which is exactly why it draws little from the state. Low state funding is a feature of that model, not evidence of deprivation. Counting all sources, the district’s own spending reached $20,508 per student in 2024–25, above the state average the Yes campaign cites. The lower figure counts state and federal dollars alone and leaves out the local property and parcel taxes that do the real funding.

Our four high schools rank among the top 5 percent nationwide. Without Measure W, drastic cuts will continue and students will suffer.

They reached that ranking on the taxes we pay now. A top-5-percent result is the strongest evidence that the current budget is sufficient to deliver an excellent education. If that standing depended on the next parcel tax, the district would not already be there.

AUHSD’s last new funding measure was 16 years ago, and the district has cut $4.2 million in the last two years alone.

The district put a new parcel tax on the ballot 18 months ago and lost. It collects $301 per parcel every year with no expiration, it received a one-time state grant of nearly $5 million in the current budget, and its property tax revenue keeps climbing with assessed values. Where the district cuts is a choice the board is making, not a shortage the taxpayers created.

Mandatory taxpayer protections: no funds for administrators’ salaries, Citizens’ Oversight and independent audits, and all funds stay local.

Money is fungible. A parcel tax earmarked for teachers frees existing dollars for everything else, including the central administration the “no administrator salaries” clause is meant to reassure you about. And oversight of this kind did not prevent the $2.1 million in out-of-state Utah special education contracts described above, one of them a placement at a facility that state had decertified.

Senior citizens are eligible for an exemption, so the tax is fair.

A parcel tax still ignores ability to pay for everyone else. It charges the owner of a small condominium the same as the owner of a mansion, and as the table above shows, these households already carry among the highest school-tax burdens in the state.
